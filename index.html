      Ethical frameworks

      Philosophy gives norms; social science explains how people react to broken exchange norms; cognitive science reminds us that moral judgment is often intuitive and emotional.

      Utilitarianism (Bentham)

      Greatest good for the greatest number. Watch for: measuring happiness, ignoring individual rights, aggregation puzzles, unforeseen consequences, and cognitive load of forecasting all outcomes.

      Deontology (Kant)

      Act only on maxims you can will as universal law. Tensions: rigidity vs. consequences, conflicting duties, difficulty defining universalizable rules, underestimation of emotion.

      Contractualism (Scanlon)

      Could you justify this to others on grounds they could reasonably accept? Challenges: aggregation, who is in the “contract,” power asymmetries, practical demands under time pressure.

      Particularism (Dancy)

      A feature that helps in one case may hurt in another. Principles are “crutches.” Risks: inconsistency, subjectivity, overlooking similarities across cases.

      Integrated analyst lens

      Blend multiple traditions—no single theory fits every deployment.

      • Utilitarian: Consider effects on wellbeing of multiple stakeholders, regardless of status.
      • Deontology: Build repeatable rules; look for what generalizes across situations.
      • Contractualism: Could you rationalize this if it were front-page news? What would PR advise?
      • Particularism: Adapt to context; state boundary conditions—if X changes, your decision may change.

      Social science · Exchange models (Fiske & Tetlock)

      • Communal sharing — undifferentiated access (e.g., clean air, family).
      • Authority ranking — order among people or goods (seniority, class).
      • Equality matching — tit-for-tat reciprocity.
      • Market pricing — ratios and trade-offs across diverse goods.

      Mixing models (e.g., pricing a communal good) causes outrage, concealment, or buck-passing. Mitigations: explicit committees for taboo trade-offs, protected bodies, open alternatives, validating discomfort.

      Cognitive science · Moral foundations (Haidt et al.)

      Judgments are often emotional and fast. Six pillars include care/harm, fairness/cheating, liberty/oppression, loyalty/betrayal, authority/subversion, sanctity/degradation.

      For analysts: classical theories map to processes you will face; broken exchange norms drive outrage and obfuscation; gut reactions matter in stakeholder meetings.

      Eight sensitive categories

      Racial/ethnic origin · Political opinions · Religion/philosophy · Trade union · Genetic · Biometric · Health · Sex life/orientation

      Consent must include…
      • Plain language; distinguishable from other text; easy access
      • Risks/benefits; voluntary participation; how to revoke
      • Controller identity; purposes and legal basis; recipients/third parties
      • DPO contact when applicable

      Transparency paradox

      More detail increases transparency on paper but can reduce reading and comprehension—seek a usable “sweet spot.”

      Privacy golden rule: Best protection is not to collect data; if you must, collect only what you need.

      Attacks & defenses

      Hacking/leaks: encryption, security—primarily engineering.

      Re-identification / linkage: quasi-identifiers + background knowledge. Mitigations: bucketing, perturbation, hashing, k-anonymity, differential privacy (centralized or decentralized).

      Sweeney (2000) — U.S. uniqueness

      High shares of people are unique from few fields (e.g., ZIP, gender, DOB). “Anonymous” data is rarely truly anonymous against a motivated adversary.

      Reference cases: public datasets re-identified via linkage (e.g., ratings + external profiles; query logs + geography). Adding noise trades utility for protection—document that trade-off.

      Sample bias & algorithmic fairness

      Selection bias (broad)

      Non-random samples skew conclusions (e.g., Literary Digest 1936; Twitter not representing the public).

      • Survivorship bias — only successes observed (e.g., “traits of billionaires”).
      • Non-response bias — who opts out systematically differs.
      • Self-selection — volunteers differ (e.g., psych study volunteers scoring higher on narcissism measures).

      Amazon hiring algorithm

      Training on past hires encoded historical bias. Dropping race/gender from inputs does not remove bias embedded in labels and proxies.

      COMPAS & ProPublica

      Black defendants flagged high-risk without re-offending at roughly twice the rate of white defendants; white defendants labeled low-risk re-offended at higher rates. Different error types carry different moral costs.

      Fairness paradox (Chouldechova, 2017)

      When base rates differ, three reasonable fairness goals cannot all hold:

      1. Calibration / accuracy — among those labeled high-risk, equal fraction actually “positive.”
      2. Equal false positives — innocent people flagged at equal rates across groups.
      3. Equal false negatives — risky people missed at equal rates across groups.

      Fixing one definition often breaks another—this is a values choice about which harm is least acceptable, not a purely technical bug.

      Systems view

      Over-policing produces arrest data that inflates apparent crime rates; feedback loops reinforce records. GDPR-compliant data can still yield discriminatory outcomes. Accountability: “The algorithm decided” does not transfer ethical responsibility.

      Pick ONE primary fairness target to optimize and document; communicate impossibility of satisfying all three when priors differ; audit who bears false positives vs. false negatives.

      Rigorous analysis & integrity

      Chronological hypothesis testing limits “researcher degrees of freedom.” Violations (p-hacking, HARKing) inflate false positives—especially seductive with big data and stakeholder pressure.

      1. Exploration (almost anything goes—then stop and pre-commit)
      2. State null and alternative hypotheses
      3. Design experiment: variables, controls, interactions, sample size
      4. Choose test statistic, alpha (Type I rate), plan
      5. Collect data per protocol
      6. Analyze; run tests
      7. Interpret—tolerate ambiguous null results

      Type I vs Type II

      Alpha = tolerated chance of false positive (declaring an effect when none exists). Trade-offs mirror policy: is a false “drug works” worse than missing a real effect?

      Anti-patterns — avoid these

      • Endless exploration; maximal flexible design
      • HARKing—hypothesize after results known
      • Cherry-pick trials or drop data
      • Peek until significant; tweak outliers, missingness, specs

      Ways to reduce false positives

      • Pre-register; fix stopping rules
      • Report all variables collected; document deletions (e.g., outliers)
      • Share data and methods; replicate
      • Bonferroni / alpha correction (watch remaining degrees of freedom)

      Replication research: a single study rarely gives definitive certainty—progress comes from reducing uncertainty over time.

      LLMs under subtle prompts can mimic “specification search” and inflate significance—treat automated analysis as needing the same constraints as human analysis.

      AI risk & structured foresight

      Doomers

      Existential risk; pause or stop.

      Gloomers

      Near-term harms; heavy regulation.

      Zoomers

      Maximize speed; minimal rules.

      Bloomers

      Transformative but iterative; public engagement + smart guardrails.

      Gloomy risk themes (expand)
      • Safety: misalignment, lethal autonomy
      • Work: displacement, inequality, deskilling
      • Bias: hiring, credit, COMPAS-scale effects
      • Privacy: surveillance, facial recognition
      • Info: deepfakes, epistemic fragmentation
      • Governance: black boxes, liability, regulatory lag

      Structured forecasting (Tetlock-style) — core practices

      1. Triage to questions where effort pays off
      2. Decompose into knowable vs unknowable
      3. Balance inside vs outside view (base rates first)
      4. Update beliefs gradually on new evidence
      5. Map clashing causal forces (“dragonfly eye”)
      6. Use granular probabilities, not vague words
      7. Balance humility and decisiveness
      8. Postmortems on failures and successes
      9. Team perspective-taking and precision questioning
      10. Practice with unambiguous feedback loops

      Explainable AI (XAI) & accountability

      Transparency tools help people interrogate decisions—they do not, by themselves, guarantee fairness, legality, or good outcomes. Pair explanations with governance, testing, and clear ownership.

      Who needs the explanation?

      Pick a primary audience—the app highlights matching practices.

      Interpretability vs. explanation

      • Intrinsic interpretability — sparse linear models, shallow trees, explicit rules: structure is readable by design.
      • Post-hoc explanation — approximations of a complex model’s behavior for an instance or cohort (e.g., local surrogates, attributions).

      Methods toolbox (tap to expand)

      LIME · local surrogate

      Fits a simple interpretable model near a prediction to explain that instance. Sensitive to perturbation design and neighborhood—can be unstable across nearby points.

      SHAP · Shapley-style attributions

      Assigns each feature a contribution based on game-theoretic averaging over coalitions. Useful for comparisons; still depends on modeling assumptions and computational choices.

      Counterfactual / contrastive

      “What minimal change would flip this outcome?”—often actionable for affected users. Must check feasibility and whether the model surface is well-behaved.

      Global / partial dependence

      Shows average prediction vs. a feature (marginal effects). Can hide interactions; combine with cohort analysis and fairness metrics.

      Law & rights (GDPR-style)

      Article 22: meaningful information about the logic involved, plus safeguards—especially when decisions are solely automated and produce legal or similarly significant effects. Explanations support transparency, not a substitute for substantive review or appeal rights.

      Accountability limits

      • Explanations can be approximate or unfaithful to the deployed model.
      • “Explainable” does not imply fair—bias can live in labels and features.
      • Documentation (model cards, datasheets) shifts responsibility to process—still need owners and redress.
